## Phase 0 — mini study brief (sourced facts; uncertainty flagged)

1. **Movable type is NOT Gutenberg's invention.** Clay movable
   type was made in China by Bi Sheng around 1041–1048 (Northern
   Song; recorded by Shen Kuo). Korea printed with metal movable
   type by the 13th–14th century — the oldest surviving book
   printed with metal movable type is the *Jikji* (1377). The
   script therefore credits China and Korea FIRST (L2) and never
   calls Gutenberg the inventor of printing.
2. **Gutenberg's contribution (~1440s, Mainz, Germany)** was the
   *system*: durable metal type, oil-based ink, and a screw press
   combined into fast, repeatable production. The Gutenberg Bible
   followed around 1455 (~180 copies). ⚠️ The exact year is
   approximate — "around fourteen forty" is the honest form, and
   the script uses it.
3. **Before the press, European books were hand-copied** by
   scribes — a single book could take months — and hand copies
   accumulate errors copy over copy. After the press, output
   exploded: by 1500, presses across Europe had produced millions
   of books (the incunabula era). ⚠️ "A thousand rooms" (L6) is a
   scale *picture*, not a count: one press run was hundreds of
   copies, but those copies were read aloud and passed hand to
   hand, reaching far more listeners than buyers. Flagged here,
   not buried.

## Narrative declaration (N-04 / N-06 / N-03 — written BEFORE the draft)

- **Structure: Problem–Agitation–Solution** (urgency). PROBLEM:
  books are copied by hand (L3). AGITATION: every copy drifts —
  errors are inherited (L4). SOLUTION: the press stamps exact
  copies, author-free (L5–L7). Deliberately NOT the series'
  And–But–Therefore spine — see Variation.
- **Intended feeling:** the slow ache of hand-copying first, then
  the clean mechanical relief of the stamp — and a small chill:
  the author is no longer in the room.
- **Villain (N-06), named: The drifting hand-copy.** Consequence
  stack: (1) a tired scribe skips a line → the mistake is copied
  into the next book and the next, with no original to check
  against; (2) each book is one of one — months of work, locked
  in one room, dying with its shelf if fire or flood comes
  (single copy = Ep.1's "no backup" in paper form). Dead
  solutions the audience already knows: copying *more carefully*,
  and employing *more scribes* — both failed because the error
  rate lives in the hand itself, and more hands just drift in
  more directions.
- **The ONE most vivid detail, chosen on purpose (N-03):** the
  skipped line — one tired moment, inherited forever. (Pattern +
  the human flaw a viewer can feel in their own handwriting.)
- **Jargon push (one deliberate push only):** "the press" is
  explained by what it *does* (stamps, L5) at first use; no other
  jargon. "Archive" returns only inside the bookend (L8), a word
  the series has already taught.

## The one thing the viewer learns (T-17)
"Around 1440, printing let a book be copied exactly, hundreds of
times, with no scribe re-copying it and no author present — so a
book could speak to thousands of people its writer never met."

Essence (≤8 words): **The book outran its author.**

## Script draft (9 lines, 121 words — counted mechanically)

1. Around fourteen forty, in a German workshop, Gutenberg finishes a machine.
2. Korea and China had moving letters centuries earlier. He makes them fast.
3. Before him, books are copied by hand — one scribe, one book, months.
4. And every hand-copy drifts: one skipped line is copied into the next book.
5. The press stamps the same page, exactly the same, hundreds of times.
6. The book speaks in a thousand rooms its author will never enter.
7. Say it plainly: printing copied information with no hand, and no author, in the room.
8. The archive no longer needed a body or a voice — just a shelf.
9. [Bridge, after the close — T-16] One chapter of how information evolved on Earth. Subscribe, like, and follow Earth One — the rest is coming.

## Delivery variation tested (vs Ep.1 control + Ep.2–4)
1. **Structure: Problem–Agitation–Solution** instead of the
   series' And–But–Therefore spine (Ep.3 also tests PAS, but in
   an *episode*; this tests PAS in the shorter teaser form and
   in a historical, non-prehistoric setting).
2. **First named person + first machine:** every prior piece
   used anonymous figures and living carriers. This tests
   whether a named historical actor (Gutenberg) and a moving
   machine hold attention better — including ONE animated
   action (the stamp at L5) against the series' push-in-only
   motion.
3. **Runtime discipline:** at 121 words it is the shortest
   script in the current batch — testing whether the Ep.1
   shape survives compression to ~63 s without losing the
   plain statement or the bookend.

## Honest weak points
1. **L2 is the most fragile line.** "Korea and China had moving
   letters centuries earlier. He makes them fast." compresses
   two different inventions (Chinese clay type; Korean metal
   type) and Gutenberg's whole system into "fast." Accurate in
   direction, thin in texture — the first line a critique round
   should attack if Hadi wants the history weighted differently.
2. **The villain is a process, not a character.** "The drifting
   hand-copy" passes N-06 by naming + stacking, but it has no
   face; the scribe at L3 is a victim of it, not the villain.
   If any teaser tests a *person-shaped* obstacle, it is not
   this one.
3. **"A thousand rooms" is rhetoric wearing a number.** Flagged
   in Phase 0 (one run = hundreds of copies; reach grew through
   re-reading and reading aloud). A strict reviewer could demand
   "a hundred rooms." Kept at a thousand for the word picture;
   named here so the choice is deliberate.
4. **The teaser jumps ~5,000 years past Ep.4.** Within the
   teaser doctrine that is the point (different parts of the
   series), but a cold viewer meeting teasers out of order gets
   no bridge between cave marks and printing. The L9 bridge
   ("one chapter") is the only stitch — thin, and stated.
