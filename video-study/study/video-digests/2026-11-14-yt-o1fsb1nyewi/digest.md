### 1b. Category brief — the "counted-vote" political explainer (enumeration-argument analysis)

**The category, named precisely:** same-day political analysis in which an unresolved institutional contest — a leadership vote, a speakership fight, a whip count — is explained by *enumerating the electorate*: every voter named, classified from the public record, and summed against a known threshold, with the analyst's own prediction separated from the count and labeled as a guess. Its shelf-mates are election-night whip-count segments and "who has the votes?" explainers; this specimen is the one-analyst, fully-animated end of that shelf (no desk, no panel, no live footage beyond recreated press cards).

**The category's format contract with its viewer:**
1. **The electorate and the threshold are knowable, and stated up front.** 55 ballots, 28 to win — the viewer is given the complete arithmetic frame in the first 30 seconds and can audit every later claim against it.
2. **Every unit in the count is earned from the record.** A senator enters a column because of something they said or did publicly, cited to its source (an outlet, an interview, a quote). Unsourced placement is the category's cardinal sin; this specimen cites in nearly every sentence (§4.3).
3. **Uncertainty is priced inside the number, not around it.** Columns carry confidence tiers (firm / soft / private), and the unknowable layer (a secret ballot's private commitments) is named as unknowable — the count's honesty *is* its credibility.
4. **The analyst's guess is separated from the count and derived in the open.** The viewer must be able to recompute the prediction from the count plus stated assumptions — here, literally: 18 + 2 recruited newcomers + "a little more than half of the other 26" → mid-30s [14:49–15:00].
5. **The outcome is pre-interpreted.** Before the vote happens, the video tells the viewer what each possible number will *mean* (the threshold ladder, T20), so the follow-up news lands on prepared ground — and the video promises an update before the vote (T22), making itself an episode in a count that continues.

**What a topic in this category must do to earn attention:** present a genuinely undecided, near-term, countable contest; open on the contradiction the count exists to resolve (two claims that "can both be true, and nobody outside can check either one" [00:13]); and let the enumeration itself be the plot — each chapter must move the count or change what the count means. A topic whose units can't be individually sourced, or whose threshold is unknown, cannot carry this form.

**Category fit of this specimen (priced fully in §6):** a nearly pure instance — the count is the outline, the tiers are labeled, the guess is derived, the ladder is drawn. Its characteristic category weaknesses appear in the delivery layer (flat within-section dynamics, §5.3) and the caption layer (the count's own names garbled by ASR, §4.4) rather than in the argument.

### P3-T01 — Dueling-claims cold open with instant undecidability verdict [0:00–0:18]
**Mechanics:** Three sentences stage the whole contest as a contradiction: a senator "announced he's running for Chuck Schumer's job" on live TV [00:00]; "Schumer replied within minutes that he has the votes" [00:06]; the challenger "says he has more than 10" [00:09]. Then the verdict that creates the video's necessity: "Those two claims can both be true, and nobody outside can check either one." [00:13]
**Why it works:** The hook is not a question but an *epistemic jam* — both claims unfalsifiable from outside — and the video positions itself as the only way through: count what can be counted.
**Transfer:** Open any counted-vote explainer on the two most contradictory public claims about the count, then state in one sentence why neither can be checked. On a speakership fight: the frontrunner's "I have the votes" against the holdouts' "he's short" — same jam, same door.
**Compression:** Survives whole — it is three sentences. In a short, this *is* the hook.
**Evidence:** transcript [00:00]–[00:18]; ev/t2.jpg (the recreated MS NOW "The Briefing" frame the announcement arrives in).

**Transfer:** Tease the precedent's *score line* in the hook (believed X, got Y), pay the full story at the midpoint. Any counted contest has one: the last time the count was wrong in public.
**Compression:** The score line survives (one sentence); the full midpoint payment is long-form-only.

## 6. Goods and bads

### 6a. Goods (each tied to evidence)

1. **The count is the outline.** Every chapter moves the tally or changes its meaning: 18 built (C3), 9 decomposed (C4), 28 segmented (C5), all of it repriced by 1971 (C6), contested through the pitches (C7–C9), embodied in one ballot (C10), pre-interpreted (C11), and verdicted (C12). 3. **The precedent is structural, not decorative** (T03/T15): teased by score line in the hook [00:41–00:53], paid whole at the midpoint [07:52], and cashed as the closing rhyme [15:21]. One historical story does the work of hook, midpoint engine, and ending.

**What the length BUYS (beats that only work with runtime):**
