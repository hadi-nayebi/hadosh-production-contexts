## 2. Consumer + production surface

**Consumer: the DLC assembler — word-animation containers.** The assembler's word-animation containers currently key every onset to a spoken-word timestamp: a word appears, scales, or highlights when the narration says it. This packet gives that container family a second, independent onset source and teaches it in Observe:

- **Collision-as-onset:** an onset can be a *filtered physical impact* — the moment a falling word-body strikes a floor or another word — and the same event that moves the word also **rewrites its content** (falling "Weee" → "Ouch" / "Thud" / "Yikes" on impact).
- **The significance filter is the transferable core:** raw contacts are far too dense to read; two thresholds (minimum spacing in time, minimum slider delta) decide which impacts are allowed to change the word. Any DLC use of impact onsets needs the same filter or the text flickers at every contact.
- **The seeded-selection rule:** the replacement word is chosen by a deterministic seeded random keyed to the accepted-impact count, so the animation is reproducible render-to-render — a production requirement the assembler's own determinism rules share.

Secondary consumer, named for the hand-off: the **studio lane** (openings — the falling-word cold open is an opening-container candidate). "General inspiration" is not claimed for anyone.

## 3. Technique catalogue

Namespaces (A7 / Cycle-012 precedent): **[SYS]** the system taught · **[TUT]** the tutorial's craft · **[PRD]** the embedded finished piece. Mechanism separation (P-A1): Newton's solver response, the exported contact stream, and the expression's rewrite are priced as three distinct parts, never merged.

### [SYS] — the system

**T01 — Collision-as-onset source-text replacement.** *Timestamps: tutorial 1:37–2:23 (expression applied); final piece throughout, first impacts by ~0:03.* Mechanics: a text layer is simulated as a rigid body in Newton; when the body collides, an expression on its Source Text replaces the word it displays. The falling state displays one fixed word ("Weee" — see the discrepancy priced in §5, B2); each *accepted* impact (T03's filter) advances a counter, and the counter selects the next displayed word from a 12-word reaction list. The body's motion (bounce, tumble, settle) is Newton's; the content change is the expression's — the two are synchronised only through the shared contact stream (T02). Why it works: the word *narrates its own physics* — the viewer reads the impact twice, once as motion and once as language, and the reaction word lands at the exact frame the eye registers the hit. Evidence: `ev/f10-first-impacts.jpg` (falling "Weee" instances above; a settled heap of reaction words — "Whoa", "Thud", "Ouch", "Yikes", "Aaah" — below; the two states in one frame) · `clips/impact-word-swaps.mp4` (8–13 s: swaps at impacts, in motion) · transcript [00:01:37] "Here's how the expression works."

**T02 — Export Contacts: collisions exported as a keyframed slider (the event stream).** *Timestamps: tutorial 0:52–1:37.* Mechanics: in Newton, a ground body is added; on the text body, Body Properties → Advanced → **Export Contacts** is enabled before solving. Newton exports to a new composition a "Contact" effect — a slider whose keyframes sit at the collision times, whose values carry the contact's energy ("Newton also tracks the energy of the contact", transcript [00:01:33]). This converts a physics simulation into a *data track* any expression can read — the bridge between the solver and the text. Why it works: it decouples simulation from presentation; the expensive solve happens once, and everything downstream is cheap keyframe reading. The exported sim comp in the tutorial is 1080×1080 at 30.00 fps, 20 s — so the `frameDuration` the expression's thresholds are built from is that comp's 1/30 s, a fact the packet states from the frame record, not from the recording's 60 fps. Evidence: `ev/t78-exported-sim-comp.jpg` (the exported "Falling_Sim_01" comp in the Project panel: 1080×1080, 30.00 fps, 20 s) · `ev/t70-newton-gravity.jpg` (Newton Global Properties at the ground-setup stage: Gravity magnitude 10.0, direction 90.0) · transcript [00:01:02] Export Contacts enabled · [00:01:23] "called Contact with a slider and keyframes that match the collisions" (verbal claim bound to the transcript record, A4 cl.3).

**T03 — The significance filter (spacing + delta + start gate).** *Timestamps: article Step 3 (printed expression); tutorial 1:37–1:57.* Mechanics, from the printed expression verbatim: iterate the Contacts slider's keys; skip any key before `startTime = inPoint + 10 × frameDuration`; accept the first remaining key; accept a later key only if it is at least `minSpacing = 3 × frameDuration` after the last accepted key **and** its slider value differs from the last accepted key's value by more than `minDelta = 30`. Why it works: a tumbling body generates contacts in bursts (chatter); without the spacing term the word would strobe through the list, and without the delta term resting-contact jitter would count as new impacts. The filter is what makes the rewrite *legible* — it prices an impact by significance, not occurrence. Evidence: `ev/t105-expression-editor.jpg` (the expression open in the tutorial, lines 1–35 legible: `slider`, `minSpacing = 3 * frameDur`, `minDelta = 30`, the words list, the acceptance condition at line 27) · article Step 3 code block. **UNVERIFIED:** the slider's units and what the delta physically measures (the narration says Newton "tracks the energy of the contact"; the expression compares raw slider values) — printed as unverified, not glossed. The composition's `frameDuration` is a property of the After Effects comp; it is *not* the tutorial recording's 60 fps, and this packet never equates them.

**T04 — Seeded word selection — and its unreachable first entry.** *Timestamps: article Step 3; tutorial 1:37–2:23.* Mechanics: `count` = number of accepted impacts so far; `seedRandom(count, true)` makes the choice a deterministic function of the count; the index is `1 + Math.floor(random(words.length - 1))` over the 12-word list `["Ow!", "Ouch", "Ugh", "Aagh", "Yow!", "Whoa", "Thud", "F*ck", "Sh*t", "D*mn", "Crap", "Yikes"]`. Because the same count always seeds the same word, successive accepted impacts *can* select the same word twice — the accepted-event count and the visible-rewrite count are different numbers, and this packet never reports one as the other (P-A3). Why it works: determinism — the piece renders identically every time, and a director can audition the exact sequence. The index formula's defect is priced as a bad (§5, B1). Evidence: the printed expression (article Step 3; `ev/t105-expression-editor.jpg` lines 7–9 show the list head and `startTime`).

**T05 — Mask-defined collision geometry for type.** *Timestamps: tutorial 0:28–0:52.* Mechanics: the text layer's anchor point is centred and a mask is drawn around the word; the narration prices why: the mask "keeps Newton from interpreting the full text geometry" and, since fonts cannot be animated directly in Newton, it gives the body a defined rectangular space to collide with. The word you *see* is type; the body that *collides* is the mask box. Why it works: letterforms have holes and concavities that make simulation unstable and unreadable; a box body tumbles cleanly and the eye accepts the word as the body. Evidence: tutorial net frames `frames/tut004.jpg` (the "Weee" comp, mask stage) · transcript [00:00:37]–[00:00:52].

### [TUT] — the tutorial's craft

**T06 — Validate on one layer before scaling (the 100-frame test).** *Timestamps: article Step 2; tutorial 0:28–1:37.* Mechanics: the entire pipeline — one text layer, floor, Export Contacts, expression — is built and proven on a single layer in a short test before any duplication. The article prices the test at 100 frames. Why it works: every failure mode of the system (no contacts exported, filter too strict, wrong slider) is diagnosable on one body; at 50 bodies none of them would be. Evidence: article Step 2 · transcript sequence [00:00:28]–[00:01:37] (single-layer construction only; duplication does not appear until [00:02:23]).

**T07 — Procedural scale-up: duplicate ×50, sequence, randomize everything.** *Timestamps: tutorial 2:23–3:32.* Mechanics, in the narration's order: duplicate the proven layer 50 times [00:02:23]; offset the copies with Sequence Layers [00:02:29] so entrances stagger; randomize scale 20–120% [00:02:36]; relaunch Newton [00:02:40]; Randomizer distributes positions [00:02:46] and rotation −10° to +10° [00:02:57]; add gravity [00:03:05]; randomize density, friction, bounciness, and angular velocity [00:03:11–3:21] — the Randomizer dialogs are shown on screen (Density; Angular Velocity −1.0 to 1.0). Why it works: variety is *parameterized*, not hand-animated — 50 performances from one setup, each physically distinct, none keyframed. Evidence: `ev/t185-randomizer.jpg` (Randomizer dialog) · `frames/tut015.jpg` (the duplicated layer stack) · transcript cues as cited.

**T08 — AI-assisted construction, shown on screen.** *Timestamps: tutorial 0:00–0:28 (word list); 1:37–2:23 (expression).* Mechanics: the 4–5-letter reaction-word list was built with an AI assistant (narration [00:00:09]: "a custom expression built with help from an AI assistant"; [00:00:11] the list brief — short words expressing pain or impact, mixing onomatopoeia, pain sounds, and soft swears); the expression itself is shown open in a ChatGPT "Slider Script" view while the narration explains it. The tutorial treats the AI as a *component supplier* (a list, a code block) inside a human-directed build — and shows the seams instead of hiding them. ### [PRD] — the finished piece

**T09 — The free-fall hold: one word, unchanged, until physics speaks.** *Timestamps: final piece 0:00–~0:03 (first landings); the hold recurs for every late-entering layer.* Mechanics: before its first accepted impact, every layer displays the same word ("Weee" — the falling sound, spelled per the expression; see B2). Because layers are offset in time and position, at any early moment the frame mixes falling "Weee" instances with already-landed reaction words. Why it works: the single repeated word makes the *change* the event — the viewer's eye is trained in the first seconds to watch for the swap. Evidence: `ev/f2-fall.jpg` (t = 2 s: "Weee" instances falling; first reaction words already at the floor) · `frames/f004.jpg` (t = 3 s, viewed firsthand: one "Weee" mid-fall; "Ugh" and "Yikes" landed below) · `clips/fall-weee-hold.mp4`.

**T10 — The accumulation heap: impacts settle into a pile that stays.** *Timestamps: final piece ~0:03–0:25.* Mechanics: landed words do not exit; bodies come to rest on the floor and on each other, so the lower frame fills with a growing heap of reaction words at mixed scales and rotations while new "Weee" instances keep falling through the upper frame. By 0:23 the heap is a dense field, its largest foreground words ("D*mn", "Sh*t") at the top of the frame, "Yow!" and "Whoa" large within it. Why it works: the piece's history is *visible* — every impact that ever happened is still on screen as a settled word; the ending is the accumulation itself, no title card needed. Evidence: `ev/f16-heap-mid.jpg` (t = 16 s: heap formed, falls continuing) · `ev/f23-final-pile.jpg` (t = 23 s: the closing pile) · `frames/f008.jpg` (t = 7 s, viewed firsthand: heap nucleated at the floor, "Weee" still falling above).

**T11 — Event-driven density ramp (the pacing is the physics).** *Timestamps: final piece 0:00–0:25.394.* Mechanics: the piece's information density is not edited; it is the integral of the impact rate. Sparse at 0:00 (single falling words), first landings by ~0:03, a nucleated heap by ~0:07, continuous accumulation to ~0:21, and a settled dense field for the last ~4 s. The *rate of new-word appearance* is set by layer offsets + gravity + the T03 filter — pacing controls that live in the simulation parameters, not in a timeline. Evidence: the 1 fps census `frames/f001–f025.jpg` read as a sequence (contact sheet viewed firsthand) · §4 tempo map.

## 5. Goods and bads

### Goods
- **G1 — Reproducibility is designed in (tutorial 1:37–2:23).** `seedRandom(count, true)` makes every word choice a function of the impact count: the piece is auditable and re-renderable, a property most physics-driven pieces give up.
- **G2 — The filter shows its working (article Step 3; tutorial 1:37–1:57).** The full expression is printed and explained — thresholds named, acceptance condition legible on screen (`ev/t105-expression-editor.jpg`). A learner can rebuild the system from the page alone.
- **G3 — One-layer validation before scale (tutorial 0:28–1:37).** The 100-frame single-layer test (T06) is a transferable production discipline, demonstrated in the piece's own construction order.
- **G4 — The ending is earned by the system (final piece 0:21–0:25.394).** The settled heap is a conclusion the physics reaches; no end card, no logo slam — the accumulation *is* the final frame (`ev/f23-final-pile.jpg`).
- **G5 — Legibility survives density (final piece ~0:10).** At mid-piece the heap is dense yet individual words stay readable — short words, heavy weight, generous scale range (20–120%) keep the field parseable (`ev/f10-first-impacts.jpg`).

### Bads
- **B1 — "Ow!" can never appear (expression, article Step 3).** The selection index `1 + Math.floor(random(words.length - 1))` ranges over indices 1–11 of the 12-word list; index 0, "Ow!", is unreachable — dead code in the list's first slot, invisible unless the formula is read closely. Priced from the printed code (A4 cl.3 evidence form: the code text).
- **B2 — The falling word's spelling disagrees across the specimen's own faces.** The expression returns `"Weee"` (3 e's); the article's prose writes "Weeee" and the narration says "Weeee" [00:00:25] — while the rendered piece itself shows "Weee" (`ev/f2-fall.jpg`). Three faces, two spellings; a learner copying the prose into the expression changes the piece. Printed as a discrepancy, not harmonised.
- **B3 — The reaction list is profanity-bearing — a consumer-surface risk (final piece, throughout; e.g. `ev/f10-first-impacts.jpg`).** Four of the twelve words are masked swears ("F*ck", "Sh*t", "D*mn", "Crap"). For this lane's consumer — *children's language-learning shorts* — the list cannot transfer as-is; the mechanism transfers, the vocabulary must be rebuilt per audience. A packet that taught the list without this flag would mislead its consumer.
- **B4 — The core dependency is a paid third-party plugin (tutorial 0:52 onwards).** Newton is commercial (the page links aescripts.com); nothing in the system works without it, and neither the page nor the tutorial prices the dependency, an alternative, or a fallback. The technique's build cost (§6) carries this.
- **B5 — Threshold semantics are asserted, not explained (tutorial 1:37–1:57).** `minDelta = 30` is given without units or a sensitivity demonstration: no rejected contact is shown beside an accepted one in the tutorial, so a learner cannot tell what 30 *means* or how to tune it for a different word length, comp size, or frame rate. The filter is the system's heart and its least-evidenced parameter. (This packet's own attempt to show a rejected contact in the finished piece's census is priced in the production record: at 1 fps the accepted/rejected distinction is not resolvable — the absence prints here, per P-A2.)

## 6. Lessons mapped to our containers

Each lesson: container · the change it implies · build cost · B1 class + consumer (DLC assembler — word-animation containers, unless named otherwise).

- **L1 — Collision-as-onset for word landings.** Container: **word animation**. Change: a vocabulary word that "arrives" (drops into a sentence, lands on its slot) can carry its own reaction — on landing it swaps to its spoken/emphasised form. Cost: **moderate** — the onset logic is an event + a filter; the physics can be a simple deterministic bounce in our HTML containers, no Newton licence needed for the *pattern* (B4 prices the original's dependency, not the pattern's). Class: technique.
- **L2 — The significance filter is a named, tunable component.** Container: **word animation**. Change: any event-driven text change ships with two printed thresholds (minimum spacing, minimum signal delta) and a start gate — never with raw event wiring. Cost: **cheap** — it is ~15 lines and a discipline. Class: trick.
- **L3 — Seeded selection for reproducible variety.** Container: **word animation**. Change: where a container picks among variants (reaction words, emphasis styles), pick by a seed keyed to the event count, so renders are reproducible and reviewable. Cost: **cheap**. Class: trick.
- **L4 — One fixed "in-flight" state word.** Container: **word animation / opening**. Change: a word in transit holds a single expressive placeholder (the falling "Weee") that trains the viewer to watch for the change at landing. Cost: **cheap**. Class: storytelling pattern.
- **L5 — Type as a physical material.** Container: **word animation**. Change: words can carry density/bounce/spin parameters; a heavy word lands dead, a light word bounces — physical character as a meaning channel (a "heavy" word in a sentence about weight). Cost: **moderate** — parameter plumbing per word instance. Class: texture.
- **L6 — The accumulation ending.** Container: **ending**. Change: an ending can be the *settled state of the piece's own events* — every word that landed stays, and the final frame is the pile — instead of a designed end card. Cost: **cheap** where the piece already drops words in. Class: storytelling pattern.
- **L7 — Event-driven pacing.** Container: **transition / word animation**. Change: density ramps can be produced by event rates (offsets + a filter) rather than by editing — a section that must feel busier raises its event rate, not its cut rate. Cost: **moderate**. Class: pacing principle.
- **L8 — Validate the pipeline on one instance, then scale procedurally.** Container: **(production method — all containers)**. Change: assembler practice: prove a new container behaviour on one word, in a short test, before batch-applying it with randomized parameters. Cost: **cheap** — a working rule, priced from T06/T07. Class: technique. Consumer: DLC assembler (production surface: its build workflow).
- **L9 — Show the seams of AI assistance.** Container: **(tutorial craft — studio lane)**. Change: where AI supplies a component (a list, a code block), the piece shows it as a component inside a directed build — a model for how our own Academy tutorials present harness-built parts. Cost: **cheap**. Class: transcript device (the narration names the AI's role at [00:00:06] and the screen shows the tool) — priced PRESENT on the transcript record (P-A6 cure: Observe's UNDETERMINED resolved at Execute from the subtitle track + frames). Consumer: studio lane — tutorial/explainer surface.
- **L10 — The word list is an audience decision, not a default.** Container: **word animation**. Change: reaction vocabularies are built per audience (B3: the specimen's list is profanity-bearing; a children's list needs impact words without swears — "Bonk", "Pow", "Oops", "Thud" survive the filter). Cost: **cheap**. Class: technique.
